Does My Car Need ADAS Calibration After a Windshield Replacement?

Short answer: almost certainly yes — if your car was built after 2017. Here's why it matters, what happens if you skip it, and what to expect from the process in Los Angeles.

You just had your windshield replaced. The glass shop did a clean job, the crack is gone, and you're ready to get back on the road. Then they hand you a piece of paper saying your car needs ADAS calibration before the safety systems will work properly again.

If you've never heard that term before, it sounds like an upsell. It isn't. This article explains exactly what's happening, which vehicles are affected, and why skipping calibration is a genuine safety risk — not just a technicality.

What Is ADAS and Why Is It in Your Windshield?

ADAS stands for Advanced Driver Assistance Systems. It's the umbrella term for the collection of safety technologies now standard on most new vehicles — things like automatic emergency braking, lane departure warning, lane keep assist, adaptive cruise control, and traffic sign recognition.

Most of those systems rely on a forward-facing camera mounted at or near the top of the windshield, looking out through the glass at the road ahead. When you replace the windshield, that camera comes out and goes back in. Even if it's reinstalled perfectly by hand, the angle is never quite what it was from the factory — and "quite" is the wrong word when we're talking about a system designed to detect a pedestrian at 60 mph and trigger your brakes.

Key Point

The forward camera that powers your automatic emergency braking, lane assist, and adaptive cruise control sits behind your windshield glass. Replacing the glass disturbs the camera's angle. Calibration resets it to factory specification.

Which Vehicles Require Calibration After a Windshield Replacement?

Most of them, and the share keeps climbing. In 2016, roughly one in four vehicles required calibration after a windshield replacement. For model year 2023 and newer, it's closer to nine in ten (Safelite).

That jump tracks the spread of the underlying hardware. Automatic emergency braking is now standard on over 95% of vehicles sold in the US from model year 2023 onward, and most of those systems depend on a windshield-mounted camera.

Nearly all major automakers — Toyota, Honda, Ford, Subaru, GM, Tesla, and the European makes — specify recalibration after windshield replacement on camera-equipped vehicles. The requirement isn't a shop's judgment call; it comes from the manufacturer's own repair procedure.

If your vehicle has any of the following features, assume calibration is required after windshield replacement:

  • Automatic Emergency Braking (AEB)
  • Lane Departure Warning or Lane Keep Assist
  • Adaptive Cruise Control
  • Traffic Sign Recognition
  • Forward Collision Warning
  • Pedestrian Detection

What Are the Two Types of Calibration?

When you call a calibration shop, you may hear the terms static and dynamic. They're not interchangeable — and some vehicles need both.

Static Calibration

Static calibration is done in a controlled indoor environment. The vehicle is parked on a level surface, and a calibration target — a large board with a specific pattern — is positioned precisely in front of the camera at a manufacturer-specified distance. The technician uses calibration software connected to the vehicle to run the procedure and confirm the camera passes. This takes between 30 minutes and an hour depending on the vehicle.

Dynamic Calibration

Dynamic calibration requires the vehicle to be driven on a road with visible lane markings at a specified speed, usually between 25 and 50 mph, for a set distance. The camera self-calibrates as it processes real-world images. Some vehicles require a technician to drive with a scan tool connected to confirm the process completes successfully.

Important for Glass Shops

Many glass shops offer calibration as an add-on service. Before you accept, ask whether they're using OEM-specified targets and software for your specific make and model — or a generic aftermarket system. The difference matters for whether the calibration is manufacturer-compliant and insurer-accepted.

What Happens If You Don't Calibrate?

This is the part most people don't realize. A car that hasn't been calibrated after windshield replacement will usually still start and drive normally. The dashboard may or may not show a warning light. But the ADAS systems are operating on bad data — the camera thinks it's aimed somewhere it isn't.

The real-world consequences range from minor to serious:

  • Lane departure warnings that trigger incorrectly — or don't trigger at all when the car drifts
  • Automatic emergency braking that activates at the wrong time — or fails to activate in an actual emergency
  • Adaptive cruise control that tracks the wrong vehicle or loses the vehicle ahead
  • Insurance complications — if you're in an accident and an adjuster discovers the ADAS systems were not calibrated after recent glass work, that affects how the claim is handled

In California, insurers increasingly require documentation of post-replacement calibration as part of the claim process. A calibration report from a certified shop protects you.

What to Tell Your Glass Shop Before They Start

If you're scheduling a windshield replacement and want to handle calibration correctly, here's what to communicate upfront:

  • Ask whether calibration is included in their quote and what equipment they use
  • If they don't offer it or can't confirm OEM procedures, ask them to leave the camera bracket accessible so a calibration-only shop can follow up
  • Request that any calibration performed is documented with a report you can keep
  • If your vehicle is under warranty, confirm with your dealer whether manufacturer-approved calibration is required to maintain coverage

Glass shops that partner with a dedicated calibration provider — rather than doing it in-house with generic equipment — typically produce better results and faster turnaround. How to Check Your Specific Vehicle

If you want to verify the requirement for your exact year, make, and model, the authoritative resource is I-CAR's OEM Calibration Requirements Search, which covers 2016-and-newer vehicles and is built from manufacturer repair procedures (I-CAR RTS). It's aimed at repair professionals but is worth knowing exists.

Or you can just call us with the year, make, model, and what was serviced. We'll confirm it before you commit to anything — there's no charge for the question. We've written more about the patterns behind calibration requirements if you want to understand what generally triggers it.

The Short Version

If your vehicle has any ADAS features and you've had the windshield replaced, calibration is not optional — it's the step that makes those safety systems actually work. The procedure is straightforward, takes less than two hours, and the documentation protects you if questions come up later with your insurer. Skipping it to save time or money is a trade-off that rarely makes sense when the systems at stake are the ones designed to prevent accidents.
